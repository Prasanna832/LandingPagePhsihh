# Top 7 Phishing Red Flags You're Probably Ignoring

Most corporate security training focuses on obvious indicators: misspelled words, generic greetings, requests for passwords. But attackers have evolved. Today's phishing emails are grammatically flawless, visually indistinguishable from legitimate communications, and crafted by professionals who have studied your organization's internal tone.

Here are seven red flags that even experienced users consistently miss.

## 1. The "From" Display Name Doesn't Match the Email Address

Your email client shows the display name prominently, but the actual sending address is often hidden behind it. A message from "Microsoft Security Team" might originate from `support@microsoFt-alerts.net`. Always expand the sender field.

## 2. The Domain Was Registered Recently

Legitimate services don't launch critical security communications from domains created last week. Check WHOIS data for any unfamiliar domain. A registration date within 30 days of receiving the email is a strong signal.

## 3. Hover-Before-You-Click Doesn't Work Anymore

Attackers now use URL redirectors, cloaking services, and legitimate cloud platforms (Google Docs, SharePoint) as hop points. A URL that looks safe on hover may redirect to a malicious final destination. Use a URL detonation service before clicking.

## 4. The Request Creates Artificial Urgency

"Your account will be suspended in 24 hours." "Verify immediately or lose access." These phrases are engineered to bypass your critical thinking. Real services rarely demand action within hours via email alone.

## 5. Attachments with Unusual Extensions

`.docm`, `.xlsm`, `.lnk`, `.iso`, `.img` — these file types can execute code. Modern attacks often use ISO files to bypass Mark of the Web protections on Windows. If you didn't request the file, don't open it.

## 6. The Branding Looks Slightly Off

Attackers use pixel-perfect clones, but small inconsistencies slip through: a logo with slightly wrong colors, a font that's close but not exact, footer links that don't match the brand's actual URL structure. Trust your instincts if something feels visually wrong.

## 7. The Email References Shared Context You Didn't Initiate

Spear phishing often includes references to your company, your name, or a colleague. If an email references a meeting you didn't schedule or a document you don't remember sharing, that context may have been harvested from LinkedIn or a data breach.

## What To Do

When you encounter any of these signals, do not click, reply, or download. Forward the email to your security team and use a tool like Phish Guard AI to scan the URLs and attachments before making any decisions.

Security awareness isn't about memorizing rules — it's about building the habit of pausing before you act.
